# gragas: the architecture

This is the record of the design as of 8 Nov 2026. It is built from [the spec](spec.md) (S1 to S75) at 77a7bd8 in the companion tool, with the fixes of the independent review (architecture-review.md, 158e723) and the structural calls of the product README's "Architecture review, structural calls (7 Nov, night)" applied. The documents named in plain text here (the review, the product README and its rulings, product-frame.md, the v6 page, research/build-rules.md, research/rules-by-component.md) are in the working folder of companion 8e9aacc, not in this repository. It supersedes the v6 architecture page (D1 to D64) where section 17 says so; the v6 index, which is not in this repository, lists the numbers. Licence of everything it describes: Apache-2.0, for the engine and for the rulebook.

**Designed and built.** This document describes a target. What is built is what `layers.yaml` lists as packages and what the old `check` and `gen` do; everything else is designed, and each part says in which milestone of section 18 it is built. Section 3.3 lists every path this document names that does not exist yet, with the milestone that creates it.

## 0. What this document is

This is the internals of gragas: how the engine and the explorer are built so that the product in `spec.md` works exactly as specified, and so that the build obeys the owner's own rulebook (research/build-rules.md in the working folder, 794 rules; 791 apply under the facets of section 1.1, 464 of them active). The product spec holds no technology. Everything technical, and the phasing, lives here.

Two words stay apart in every sentence, as in the spec. **The engine** is gragas, one program. **A rulebook** is content, one repository per owner. The engine ships no rulebook for use. The one rulebook inside the engine is the fixture rulebook, the conformance suite, and nothing more.

Three bodies of text bind this design, in this order of authority: the spec (what the product does), the owner's rulebook (how the build must be made), and the valid parts of v6 (what the previous session kept). Every constraint cites its rule id in brackets. A cited rule that is proposed or later does not bind yet (section 7 of build-rules.md carries each status); the design meets it ahead of activation rather than against its grain. Where the spec and a rule disagree, section 19 names it and proposes the fix; nothing here silently bends either.

The design is made to the spec's domains (section 14), its contracts (sections 15 to 17) and its decisions, not to incidental wording, so a wording fix lands without restructuring. The spec's delta review and this document's independent review are both absorbed; what the spec still cannot say is in section 19.

## 1. gragas declared in the rulebook's terms

The owner's rulebook (schema v2) binds a product by its kinds and facets. the owner's product list has no row for gragas, and gragas's own project file declares none. This section decides both. The gragas format has kinds only (S61, S73): once the owner's rulebook is in format v3 (M6), each facet below is a kind that rulebook defines, and the engine's `.gragas/pin.yaml` lists `cli`, `web-app` and the declared facets together as its kinds, with no second field. Until M6 the engine's repository is not yet a project, and the facets stay in the schema v2 files the rulebook proposes them in.

### 1.1 Kinds and facets to declare

Kinds: `cli` (the engine, one Go binary) and `web-app` (the explorer, a React single-page app built as a static bundle). `site` is out: its only part is a framework the stack does not use. `daemon` and `service` are out: the engine runs no long-running process and answers no request (S16, S20).

### 1.2 Published packages and the `library` kind

The contract types, the web component and the two plugins are packages other code imports. Declaring `library` for the whole repository would excuse 52 rules that carry `unless library`, which is a loosening. Decision (A2): the repository declares `cli` and `web-app` only. The two rules that bind libraries and are skipped today, ts.packages.1 and supply.bumps.3, are held for the packages by a test in the repository and a recorded decision, until the rulebook can scope a kind by path. The rulebook's `unless` already takes `paths`, so the ask is a `when` with `paths` on those two rules, sent as a proposal through the rulebook's own lifecycle before M8, so the packages are bound when they exist (README ruling 7).

## 2. Bounded contexts

The spec classifies subdomains (section 3) and leaves the mapping to the architecture (S21). One bounded context per subdomain, with the core domain split into its two domains because they have different ports: the enforcement link reads configs and derives, proof runs tools. Every context is one Go package under `internal/core/`, named by what it does, and reaches the outside only through ports [arch.ports.1]. No context imports another and no context calls another: the shared value types live in the types-only leaf `internal/model` (A4), what one context needs from another arrives as an argument, and the one place the contexts meet is `internal/core/api` (package `coreapi`), one composition per command [arch.ports.8]. The independence contract is data: `layers.yaml` lists the nine contexts under `contexts:`, and the boundary lint generated from it refuses an import between any two [arch.layers.9]. Where a context needs a lazy read it declares a small interface on its own side (as the current `layers.yaml` does for the generator's `Book`), and `coreapi` passes an implementation.

The core domain is where gragas must be clearly best (product-frame.md). Both core packages are pure: no `os`, no `net`, no `exec`, no `time.Now` [arch.ports.1, arch.ports.4]. They hold no operating-system code and no platform literal [arch.ports.4, arch.ports.5].

### 2.1 What each context hands over, and how

The spec's 14.1 map draws arrows between domains. In code, no arrow is an import and no arrow is a call. A context takes model values and the port interfaces it declares as arguments, and returns model values. `coreapi` composes, in a fixed order per command: load (format), derive (link), the context's plan or listing function, render (format), then the write path (`internal/apply`, section 5). Every cross-context value is named as an argument in section 6, so a reader can see what `coreapi` passed and from where [arch.ports.8, go.style.1].

**What `coreapi` carries, and nothing else.** One file per command family, each function a composition of calls into the contexts and a hand-over to `apply`. It declares no type (every plan, delta, finding and result type is in `model`), holds no write logic (`apply` has all of it), opens no port itself (it passes members of the `port.Ports` set to the context that asks for them) and never imports `host/*`. A file in it stays under three hundred lines, held by a test, so it cannot become the place where logic hides [arch.structure.6, arch.ports.14].

| Spec arrow | In code |
|---|---|
| 1 → 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9: the parsed model | `format.Load` returns `model.Book`; `coreapi` hands it to every other context |
| 2 → 3, 5, 6: status, history, decisions | they are fields of `model.Book` after `format.Load`. Lifecycle changes them only through plans |
| 3 → 2, 5, 6, 8, 9: standing, marks, coverage, conflicts, the resolved chain | `link.Derive(book, proof, chain)` returns `model.Truth`. Attention, release, pages, check and agent files take a `model.Truth` |

## 3. Layout and the module graph

*Designed. The packages that exist today are the rows of `layers.yaml`; section 3.3 lists the rest with the milestone that creates each.*

The layout is the one go.layout.1 prescribes: the program under `cmd/gragas/`, everything else under `internal/`, no pkg folder [go.layout.1]. No package is named util, common, helpers, types or misc [arch.structure.7]. The module graph is declared in `layers.yaml`, imports run one way, there are no cycles, and the boundary lint and the repository test are generated from that file [arch.layers.1, arch.layers.2, arch.layers.8, arch.layers.9]. Every source file belongs to exactly one layer [arch.layers.3]. A module is reached only through its one public entry [arch.layers.5]. Production code never imports a stand-in or a fixture [arch.layers.10].


`layers.yaml` holds two more tables the lints are generated from [arch.layers.9]: `contexts:`, the nine context packages whose mutual imports are refused, and `explorer:`, the TypeScript workspace's modules (`contract`, `tokens`, `core/data`, `core/selectors`, `core/views/*`, `core/messages`, `standalone`, `element`, `plugin-docs`, `plugin-portal`) with their allowed imports, rendered into the ESLint boundary rule, so the explorer's modules have the same independence contract as the Go contexts [arch.ports.8]. Each library has exactly one owner row [arch.ports.6, supply.intake.1]: the CLI framework to `cli/root`, the shared terminal kit to `host/term` (its look) and `exit` (its exit leaf only), YAML to `ordered`, the Markdown parser (goldmark, A58) to `markdown`, RFC 8785 JSON (the standard library's jsontext, A59) to `canon`, JSON Schema to `host/jsonschema`, nothing else imports them. ### 3.2 Where the module graph differs from 14.1, and why

1. 14.1 draws 3 ↔ 4, 3 ↔ 5 and 2 ↔ 3 in both directions. A Go import graph cannot. Each pair becomes two data flows through `coreapi` and a shared type in `model` (section 2.1). The spec's reading "nothing outside the core computes standing" still holds: only `link.Derive` makes a `model.Truth`.

### 4.1 Tools are plugins

gragas knows no tool. It embeds no description of one, and its non-test Go names none; a gate test refuses a tool's name there. What it knows is the plugin interface, and anything that fulfils the interface works with gragas (the plugin-interface decision). A plugin is code, in the author's language, for one tool; the one in this tree is `standin`, a fake the tests use. The plugins that serve golangci-lint, ESLint, the TypeScript compiler and ast-grep live in birdtable/gragas-plugins, beside the SDK. gragas always runs the tool itself: a plugin says what to run and reads what came out, and starts nothing.

**The interface is five operations** over a versioned contract (`contract/plugin/v1/`):

- `plan` says what to run: the steps gragas is to run, what to read, and whether the plan is final. It is the one operation that is called more than once, in rounds (below).
- `entries` turns a tool's printed or read config into tool entries: tool, target, key → value, options, severity.
- `findings` turns what a run printed over a fixture into the findings it names, each mapped to a tool entry.
- `rank` says how two values of one entry compare: tighter, weaker, off or no word, and why.
- `preset` writes a tool's preset text from the bindings, with `extends` where the tool has it and copy-once (S42) where not.

What a plugin used to declare as data, its tool, its version, the tool version range it describes, the target types, the severities and the warning level, whether a docs URL can be set, and where a project's tool lives, is its manifest, plugin.yaml: its **facts** and its **version**, read before the plugin runs, and not operations. The manifest also names the plugin's **grants**: the programs it may have gragas run. A grant is one of three closed forms: the pinned places alone, a path, or a script with its `manifest`, `script_at` and `interpreter`; every grant carries its own `versions`, and `tool_versions` judges the grant whose `id` is the tool. A step names a program by grant id and never by path, and for a script gragas puts the script first in the argv, so a plugin cannot reach `node -e`. The level a step runs at is the higher of the engine's floor and the step's `level`: a plugin can raise it, never lower it. The floor of a config read is the engine's, from the config's name: a config whose extension is json, yml or yaml, of any case, is data and is read isolated, any other name sandboxed, and a read with no config isolated. A data config can still load code, a plugins key of a JSON config, say, and only the plugin knows its tool's keys, so the manifest's `config_loads_code: true` raises every config read of the tool to sandboxed, whatever its extension; left out, it is false and the floor is the name's. It only tightens: no value of it lowers a read. The Steps port (section 4) tries the settings first, then the place the grant names, then the tools file (A8).

**Plan rounds.** A plan is made in rounds: at most three plan calls, 48 steps in all and one read call. Each round, gragas checks the answer against the grants, runs the steps through the Sandbox and Steps ports, and hands what they printed back to the plan. A plan that answers `final: false` at round 2 ends at the plan's answer, before that round's steps run (gragas#2024). A round or a step beyond the budget, a step outside the grants, or an answer that does not fit the contract ends the call Failed and names the plugin; it is never read as an empty result. Refused is the plugin's own answer, that it declines its input.

**The carrier is WebAssembly.** internal/host/plugin is the one package that imports wazero, and it fills the Plugin port. A plugin is a WebAssembly module, so it reaches the world only through the calls the host gives it. Compiler mode is allowed on three conditions: no compilation cache is configured and a compile writes no file, memory is never writable and executable at once, and wazero's own fall to its interpreter is accepted. The fall-back order, should a platform refuse one, is wazero's compiler, wazero's interpreter, a child program, Starlark, then goja; which of the first two a build picks is settled by one measurement, the whole of one `check` with four plugins from a fresh process, in each mode.

**Trust is per machine, by digest and grants.** `gragas plugin add <source> <name>@<version>` plans, shows and, with `--apply`, installs a plugin into the rulebook's `plugins/<name>/<version>/` and pins the digest of that folder in its lock; it does not run it. Release 1 takes a path as the source: a fetch is ruled after v1. The first run of a plugin on a machine needs `gragas plugin approve`, which shows the grants and the version argv and records the digest on this machine; a machine with no terminal approves by digest on the command line. A change of the digest, or of one grant, is not approved until it is shown again, and `approve` shows what changed in the grants. A rulebook's or a project's plugin is fetched data and so takes no more than the grants a person approved on this machine; nothing a fetched layer carries widens them. A new version in release 1 is `plugin add` and then `plugin approve`; `plugin upgrade` comes after it. A plugin's answer is the fifth kind of less-trusted data (4.3).

**The author's side.** The SDK in birdtable/gragas-plugins is what a plugin is built with, and `gragas plugin test` runs 24 conformance checks over one: that each operation answers the contract, that a plan keeps to its budget and its grants, that a planted output maps, a clean control passes and a refusal case is refused [gates.plants.1]. A plugin ships with the recordings its tests replay through the stand-in [tests.doubles.6]. The contract's codec keeps its fuzz targets with seeds in this repository [tests.generated.1]; the output parsers' move to the SDK.

The plugins of release 1 are golangci-lint, ESLint, the TypeScript compiler and ast-grep. The prose linter and the policy tool of A55 are plugins by the same interface when they are built; a reviewer format at rung 6 (no run, an eval case only) comes in M9.

### 4.2 The two run levels

Isolated (level `i`): `host/sandbox` makes a scratch folder in the engine's cache, places each pair at the path its binding declares (S44), mirrors the config set, links the tool's `resolve-from` folder, scrubs the environment to PATH and LANG, sets a deadline, and a memory limit only where one is set, and hands the folder to Steps. The folder the scratch folders are made in is held open and judged on its handle: it must be this account's, root's refused, and go-files' `trust.Held` judges who else may write it, by its mode bits, a macOS access list, a Linux one's mask or a Windows one, and the volume it is on, so the isolated level opens on Windows, where until gragas#1607 a folder owned by a SID was refused unjudged (go-files#257); a base on a mount whose owners go-files does not judge (a FUSE volume another account mounted, one that ignores ownership, a Linux disk mounted nosuid or noexec) is refused where the mode bits alone passed it, and its fix line, host/trust's for `ErrMount`, says the way the disk is mounted is why, not the folder. So a cache on a hardened nosuid /home is refused by name until the setting of gragas#1659 names that disk, which the sandbox is to pass to `trust.Held` as `Who.Mounts`, as the trust walk is. gragas sets no memory limit of its own (#1504): a folder prepared with none holds its run to none, so Steps asks go-proc for no cap and no peak, and the run starts on every host, a Linux host with no delegated cgroup among them. What follows of memory is a run with a limit set. The tool's output is parsed and never executed. The engine runs and writes nothing through the link, but at this level nothing stops a tool writing through it, so the output calls the folder linked and never read-only; only the sandboxed level makes it so (#201). Tool holds a run to its folder's limits where it starts the child, through go-proc's `proc.Run`, the one start of `host/tool` and the one of `host/git`, held by a tree test (gragas#920): the lower of its own deadline and the folder's; a cap on the whole tree's memory at twice the memory limit (`proc.Limits.TreeMemory`, an interim figure until a measured table sets it, gragas#1450), a backstop that stops a run before it takes the machine, held by a cgroup's memory.max on a Linux host that delegates one and by the Job Object on Windows; and the peak go-proc reads when the run ends (`ReadPeak`, go-proc#73), judged against the limit. Before it starts anything, Steps asks go-proc whether this system holds the cap (`proc.Holds`): where it is held, the run goes capped; where it is not, macOS and the BSDs among them, but a peak can still be read, the run goes uncapped and is judged after it ends, and says so; where neither, a Linux host with no delegated cgroup, the run is refused by name before it starts, with the systemd setting that delegates one (Delegate=yes and DelegateSubgroup=app). What the peak counts is named with it (`Result.PeakKind`): on Linux the most memory charged to the tool's tree at once, its page cache among it; on darwin and the BSDs the larger of the tool's own resident peak and that of a descendant it waited for; on Windows the most committed to any one process of the tree. It is never this program's, and outside Linux never the sum of the processes that ran at once, so the memory limit at the isolated level is best-effort; only the sandboxed level caps the whole run everywhere. No program asks go-proc to start it as a wrapper any more: the cap is the kernel's, not a re-exec of this binary (it replaces #809's wrapper and #1149's reading). A run go-proc gives no reading of, and one whose tree held a live process when the tool exited (`Result.Left`, go-proc#78), whose peak was never read, is not counted: it fails closed by name, and the limit is never raised. The tool runs in a tree of its own, a process group on Unix and a Job Object on Windows, and go-proc ends the whole tree with the run, at its deadline, its cap, a cancel and the tool's own exit (#460), so no worker outlives the run. Every run names how long go-proc reads the tool's output after the tree is killed (`Cmd.Drain`, ten seconds, held by a tree test, gragas#1450), so a pipe a process that left the tree still holds never cuts the output short unseen. A limit hit is the cell "not run" with the limit named. A run the runner refuses before it starts is handed back refused, and is not yet journalled (gragas#1296).

The runner is a guard against accidents, not a jail, in go-proc's own words; the handbook's chapter on runs, when it is written, owes the same words. What it does not hold:

- On Unix a child that leaves its group, by a new session or a group of its own, is not reached. A run whose output such a process holds open ends with `escaped` once its pipes are closed, and the run names it.
- On Unix a process of the tree that changes its user, as `sudo` does, cannot be killed: the run ends with `unkillable`, and that process runs on.
- The exec swap: Tool checks the tool's file (its owner, and who can write it and the folders above it), and `proc.Run` then starts it by its path, so a file swapped in at that path between the check and the exec runs in its place. A start by the open file that was checked, where the system has one (`execveat` on Linux), would close it; go-proc has no such start yet (go-proc#79).

Sandboxed (level `s`): the same folder, run inside one of three adapters chosen by the settings file, each a child program whose absolute path the settings file names: a container runtime (image pinned by digest in the rulebook's tools file), a Linux user-namespace sandbox, or the macOS sandbox profile tool. Each denies network and file access outside the folder. The adapter records the level it reached; a sandboxed request on a machine with none of the three ends the cell as "not run, sandbox missing", a finding (S23). A config read asks this level for every config that is not data by its name, and for every config of a tool whose manifest says `config_loads_code: true` (4.1); release 1 has no sandboxed adapter, so such a read ends failed with the scratch folder not made, and nothing of it is imported.

Both levels keep every file under the engine's cache, never under a project's tree (S44). Proof timestamps go to the journal, not to any derived document (S57). A child program gets a bare environment, a deadline and capped output at both levels (cli.process.2 above); gragas runs each enforcer tool itself, and a plugin is not a child program that speaks a request protocol (that carrier is a fall-back, 4.1), so cli.process.3's request-object clauses have no subject for a tool.

### 4.3 Less-trusted data

Five kinds of input come from a program or a repository the engine does not control: an enforcer tool's printed config and diagnostics, a plugin's answer, a fetched release or a parent's bundle, a report file, and a signals batch. Such data reaches a decision only through fields declared decidable on its type [arch.ports.19]. Every field of a model type that carries it has a doc comment that says `decides: <the one decision>` or `display only`. For the contract's types the mark comes from the schema: a property that drives a decision carries `x-decides` naming it, gragas-generate writes "Decides: <it>." from that and "Display only." on every other field, and refuses an `x-decides` that names nothing, so a field decides only where its schema says so.

| Data | Decides | Display only |
|---|---|---|
| a tool's output | a cell's pass or fail, an observation word | message text, the tool's docs URL |
| a plugin's answer | the steps it asks gragas to run, each held to its grants, and the entries, findings and ranks it reports, which a cell and an observation word read | message text, the tool's docs URL |
| a fetched release | the digest list and the key fingerprint, which decide whether it is accepted | changelog prose, rule prose, remedies, translations |
| a report file | the fidelity finding kinds the report states, `fail-on` | free text, names |
| a signals batch | the counts it carries | everything else |

A syntax-tree test over `link`, `proof`, `project`, `release`, `coreapi` and `contract`, which reads every document first, fails a condition, a switch or its case, a comparison or a map key that reads a display-only field, and carries a planted read that must fail [arch.ports.19, gates.plants.1]. It type-checks each context against the contract types' export data, reads one that is planned once it exists, and fails a context that is neither built nor planned, or that does not type-check. The decision is A46.

## 5. The write path

*Designed, built in M0b.*

Twenty-three commands end in a plan (15.1). All of them go through one path, `internal/apply`, a pure package over the ports, so every changing screen is the same screen (S43) and the most delicate code in the engine has one home and its own tests (A35).

**A plan is a delta and its rendering.** A context decides and returns a `model.Delta`: the entities and fields that change, each with its value before and after, the record of the yes to add, and nothing about bytes. `coreapi` then calls `format.Render(book, delta)`, which returns `[]model.File`: for each file touched, its path, the digest of the bytes it replaces, the new bytes, or for `history.jsonl` the one line to append. Render edits a person-written file only in the lines it owns. In YAML form it changes the owned keys at the line positions `ordered` recorded when it parsed, and leaves every other byte, comments and order included. In Markdown form it rewrites the one fenced `gragas` block of the section and leaves every byte outside the block as it was, which is the flagship promise of S2 and S36 [cli.commands.23, store.state-files.2]. A file the engine generated whole (a preset, a bundle, the lock, the state file) is rendered whole. A round-trip golden holds this: a commented fixture edited by every lifecycle command comes back byte-identical outside the owned lines [cli.commands.23, tests.generated.5]. The `model.Plan` is the delta, the files, the sections of 15.3, the input set and the goal predicate (below), and the plan digest, a canonical hash over all of it (`canon`) (A36).

1. **Plan.** `coreapi` composes: load, derive, the context's plan function, `format.Render`. One function makes the plan for the dry run, for the terminal form and for the apply [cli.commands.17]. No operation takes a dry-run parameter. The plan records the digest of every file it read, the input set: the rule files, identity, the last line of history, the pin, the lock, the state file, the plugin's digest and grants, the tested set, the proof grid, whichever the command touched. A clock reading a plan needs (the release date, "when") is taken once at plan time and carried into the apply unchanged, so a re-plan over the same inputs has the same digest.
2. **Show.** `cli/root` renders the plan through Terminal. Without `--apply` the run ends on one footer naming the flag, then "declined", exit 6 (S43, A9) [cli.look.6]. At a terminal the form asks once, "apply?", and a yes is the apply (A9). Off a terminal without the flag nothing is asked [cli.environment.8] and the plan prints.
3. **Lock.** `apply.Run` takes the root's Lock, non-blocking. Held means "busy", exit 3, with the holder's start time [store.consistency.1, cli.commands.3]. With `--apply` in one process the lock is taken before the plan is computed, so plan and apply see one tree. After a yes at a terminal the plan was computed before the lock: `apply` runs the plan function again under the lock and compares plan digests; a different digest shows the new plan and asks again, and never applies the plan the person did not see. The open-record check of 5.2 runs here.
4. **Intent.** Journal records, before the first effect: the run id, the command, the plan digest, the whole plan (the delta and every rendered file, so recovery needs nothing else), the input set, each target's digest before, and for release the commit and the tag it will make [store.consistency.4]. A journal that cannot be written stops the apply.
5. **Check.** Every digest of the input set and of every target is read again under the lock and compared with the plan. Any difference ends "refused" before the first write, naming the file: the tree moved under the plan [cli.commands.22, store.consistency.4].
6. **Write.** Each file through Writer inside the root the command names and nowhere else [cli.commands.4, cli.commands.23]. Before a file is replaced its copy goes to the journal. The order is fixed: data files first, the record of the yes last. In a rulebook the record is one line appended to `history.jsonl` through Writer's append [store.state-files.5]; in a project it is one yes entry in the lock, rewritten whole. Each carries who, when, the plan digest and the command (S43). Release's commit and tag are steps inside this one, inside the record (5.3).
7. **Goal.** Each plan carries its goal predicate: the digest every written file must now have, the history line present as its last line, and for release the tag naming a commit whose tree holds those digests. `apply` reloads from the tree and checks the predicate; "done" only then [cli.commands.22]. A failed predicate rolls back (5.2) and ends "failed" with the run id. An empty replan is not the test, because `undo`, `decision new`, `propose` and `release` legitimately replan non-empty after a yes.
8. **Close.** Journal closes the record with each written file's copy from before and digest after [cli.commands.25]. The lock is released on every path [go.concurrency.3].

**Idempotence.** A second run with the same inputs computes an empty delta and ends "done, nothing to do" [cli.commands.2, cli.commands.6]. Retiring a retired rule, waiving an already waived path, binding an existing binding, each returns done without a write.

**Destructive steps.** `withdraw`, `archive`, `switch` and the opt-out forms of `agents` and `review` take `--confirm <name>` (the release, the rulebook, the target release, the folder removed) besides `--apply`; at a terminal the name is typed [cli.commands.3]. `rule retire`, `unbind` and every other plan are reversed by a history entry (S59) and take no confirm (README ruling 2).

**The guard in the rulebook repo.** Lifecycle fields change only through the CLI (S59). One command, `guard`, with two forms, both in the rulebook's own repository and none in a project (S14, A29):

- `guard edit` reads one proposed edit on stdin as the agent hook protocol gives it (path and new content), through Terminal's line in, and denies any direct change of a lifecycle field, full stop: "refused", exit 5, naming the field and the command to use [agents.guards.1, agents.guards.4].
- `guard staged` reads the staged change (the paths, and the index's blobs through History) and allows a lifecycle field change only when the same change appends a `history.jsonl` line whose before and after match the field change and whose plan digest the engine recomputes from the staged tree; anything else is "refused". This is how the CLI's own change passes a pre-commit hook and a hand edit of the same field does not.
- `rulebook.yaml` is not a lifecycle file: `guard staged` allows a hand edit of its `owners`, as of its name, version and governance, in a plain commit and in a merge alike (gragas#2067). The owners are display only, no command changes them after `init` and `migrate`, and a refusal would leave them with no way to change. Where an owner reaches a held value, a command records it: `import` writes the book's first owner as a grandfathered rule's `owner`, which the guard holds, with its history line. A hand-edited `owners` list that the schema refuses passes `guard staged`, which does not judge owners, and `validate` catches it.
- During a merge (MERGE_HEAD present), `guard staged` judges each commit the merge brings in as its own staged change, then holds the merge's history to the other side's lines in that side's order and its lifecycle fields to the other side's change from the merge base. A rulebook it cannot read on any side is refused.
- The lines init prints: `gragas guard staged || exit 2` for the pre-commit hook, `gragas guard edit` for the agent's settings, and `gragas guard staged --merge-hook || exit 2` for the pre-merge-commit hook, since git runs no pre-commit hook for a merge it commits itself with no conflict (gragas#1138). git runs pre-merge-commit before it writes MERGE_HEAD, so under `--merge-hook` the other side cannot be named: a merge that leaves every lifecycle value and `history.jsonl` as HEAD holds them passes, and any other is refused, so git keeps the merge and `git commit` runs the pre-commit hook, which judges it three ways. The other side is never read from GIT_REFLOG_ACTION or any other name a caller sets. A missing binary fails closed.
- The hooks are a convenience, not a control: a person can skip a hook, a rebase or cherry-pick that finishes with a plain commit is judged as one, and a fast-forward runs no hook at all, so a commit made with no hook lands unjudged when HEAD can fast-forward to it (`git merge stash@{0}`, or a branch committed with `--no-verify`; gragas#1270). The control is the gate's `check` below, which reads the merge base (#168).
- The gate: `check` in a rulebook reads the merge base through History and lists, as findings, a lifecycle field that changed without its entry, an entry of the base's chain removed or rewritten, and a history gap (S14). It judges each commit since the merge base as `guard staged` judges a commit a merge brings in, so a commit HEAD fast-forwarded to is judged as its hook would have judged it; a commit of more than two parents (a stash made with `--include-untracked`) is held to no one other side and is a finding, never a pass (`internal/journeys/check_fastforward_test.go`).

**History under branching.** A history entry carries its sequence number, its plan digest and the digest of the entry before it on the branch where it was made. Two branches each append the next number; git merges `history.jsonl` by concatenation, since every change is one new line at the end. The reader orders entries by sequence number, then "when", then plan digest; two entries that share a number and name the same parent are both valid and are not a finding. The next append after a merge takes the highest number plus one. The gate's checks run over the merge base's chain: every entry on it stays byte for byte, and a line that cannot be read is skipped, counted and listed. Other numbers a yes assigns (an exclusion's sequence number, 16.1) can collide the same way, and there the merge is refused: both sides change one exclusion's record since the merge base, no line records which record a merge keeps, and no command renumbers or removes a record (a hand renumber on either branch is a change with no line, which `guard staged` refuses). The way through is to abort the merge, drop one side's commit that takes the number, and merge again; that exclusion is then made again through `rule exclude` on the merge, which takes the next number and writes its line. A file that holds one key twice anyway, from a merge made past the guard, is a `validate` finding that names the same way (A38, gragas#1271).

**Report writes.** (`docs` in its accept form is not one: it is a plan with a yes, A54.) `prove` (the grid to `proof/grid.json` in the rulebook, a generated document the owner commits; release copies it to `release/proof.json`), `report`, `docs` and `explorer` write documents, not lifecycle data. They use the same path with no yes record: lock, intent, atomic write, goal, close. Their plan is the file set and nothing else, and the screen shows what will be written.

**The local state file.** Per-machine facts (tool versions found, last fetch, withdrawn list seen, S66) live in `.gragas/state.yaml` in each root: a project has one for its pinned release, a rulebook has one for its parent, import and upstream fetches. The file is ignored by version control, and init prints the ignore line with the wiring (A17). The state file has a freshness age the handbook names: when it is missing or older than that age, status and check fetch the withdrawn list; where they cannot, they print "withdrawn: not checked on this machine" and end done. It is never rewritten on a run that found nothing new, so it is not the lock's noise (S66). The lock, the journal and the caches stay in the engine's state folder, keyed by the root's digest. Every path and file name of persistent state is made in one function of `internal/config` [store.state-files.1, cli.config.5]. Each versioned file carries its version inside it and its reader says what it does with an older or newer one [store.schemas.2].

### 5.1 The two kinds of yes

| Context | Record | File | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| a rulebook | a history entry | `history.jsonl`, append-only | sequence number, the previous entry's digest, kind, entity, lifecycle fields before and after, who, when, plan digest, decision (16.1) |
| a project | a yes entry | `.gragas/lock.yaml`, rewritten whole | who, when, plan digest, command; the yes that took an unpinned release names the fact; the yes that allowed a rendering also records its sum in the lock's rendering sums (16.3, S66) |

"Who" comes from git's configured identity read through History, never typed; "when" is the Clock, in UTC, an authored input of the entry (S57).

### 5.2 Crashes and open records

An apply that changes several files rolls back; it never resumes from a mark [cli.commands.7]. The journal holds the plan and the copy of each replaced file, so an interrupted run leaves its root in one of two states: before the plan, or after it with a closed record. Every command, reading or writing, checks its root for an open record first. A writing command rolls the open record back from the copies, says so, and goes on with its own plan. A reading command (`status`, `check`, `validate`, `explain`, the listings) ends "not ready" naming the interrupted run and the command that recovers it: `journal recover`, or any writing command. One exception, because the commit already holds the files: a release record whose files are written and whose commit exists but whose tag does not is completed, the tag made, and the run says so. SIGINT and SIGTERM cancel through the context and roll back the same way [go.concurrency.4]. Crash injection in the gate kills a scripted apply at random points, restarts, and asserts "every root is before the plan, or after it with a closed record" [store.recovery.2] (A37). Each writing command of the tree is on the crash list, `gate/crash.json`, with a workload the crash child runs wired as `cmd/gragas` wires it (its clock fixed, one fixed account answering who commits, never a terminal, its writer and journal counted) and kills at its boundaries, a scripted crash test, or a row naming why it writes outside the write path and the issue that moves it (gragas#340, gragas#1420).

### 5.3 Release's commit and tag, inside the record

Release's plan lists, besides its files, "commit <named paths>" and "tag vX". Its intent records both. After the files (step 6), `apply` asks Remote to commit the named paths and to make the annotated tag, which is never signed: git is told on its command line not to sign it, whatever the account's or the repository's config says (release-pin-by-hash). `digests.txt` lists every file of the release by digest, and the release's content hash, which a project pins, is computed through `internal/digest`. The goal predicate is the tag naming a commit whose tree holds every written digest. The record closes after the tag. Release never pushes; it ends "done" with the one line to push [supply.release.1]. The artifacts the tag carries are in the tree (7.1), so a project fetches a release as a tag from any host, path or mirror with git alone (A11).

### 5.4 The run-level way back

The closed record keeps each changed file's copy from before and its digest after. `journal` lists the runs on a root; `journal recover` rolls an open record back (5.2); `journal restore <run>` prints the lines that put each file back, and is refused when a file's digest moved since, naming the runs that changed it [cli.commands.25]. It runs nothing. This is the run-level way back, and it is a different thing from `undo`, which is a lifecycle correction recorded as a new history entry (S59). Retention: the journal's copies, the explain index, the config cache and the releases cache are kept for the age the settings file names (thirty days by default) and are listed and cleared by `cache`; nothing in the state folder carries a project's name, only root digests [go.concurrency.7 for the hold and bound].

A run record carries its format version and the same fixed fields whoever did the work (the run id, the command, the plan digest, the root digest, the engine version); detail from a tool or the platform sits under one passed-through field, and `journal` answers are built from the record, never from the tool [cli.commands.24]. `journal restore` writes nothing, so cli.commands.28's fresh-folder rules have no subject.

### 5.5 Who writes each persisted file

`internal/config` holds a table of every persisted file (the file, the command that writes it, whether it can be rebuilt, its durability), and a test walks it against the writes `apply` makes, so a file nobody listed cannot be written [store.seams.4, store.state-files.1]. The package comment of each store-facing adapter says the same three things [store.seams.6].

| File | Written by | Can be rebuilt | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| `history.jsonl` | every plan that ends in a rulebook yes | no, and it lives in the owner's git repository | append, one line, flushed [store.state-files.5] |
| `.gragas/lock.yaml`, `pin.yaml`, `waivers.yaml`, `baselines.yaml` | the project plans | no, and they live in the owner's git repository | replaced whole, flushed [store.state-files.2] |
| `.gragas/state.yaml` | status, check, prove, upgrade | yes, by the next fetch | replaced whole |
| the run journal and its copies | `apply` | no, but it is transient: closed records are kept for the age in the settings file | append, one line, flushed; a plain, versioned, append-only file [store.seams.6] |
| the explain index, the config cache, the releases cache | `cache`-managed, written by explain, check and fetch | yes | replaced whole |
| the lock files | `apply`, `proof.Run` | not state | a kernel advisory lock, no content that matters |

Backup of the two non-rebuildable kinds is git, the owner's own. The engine owns no store that git does not carry and that cannot be rebuilt, so a restore drill has nothing to restore beyond crash injection's before-or-after invariant [store.recovery.1, store.recovery.2].

## 6. Each command's pipeline

*Designed. Each command is built in the milestone section 18 gives it.*

Every command is one `coreapi` call from a thin command file that parses flags and returns a value the root renders [cli.output.2, store.seams.1]. "R", "P", "B" and "A" are the spec's contexts. The root detects the context once: an identity file at the root means a rulebook, a pin found walking up means a project, both means both, else neither. In every pipeline below the arrows are calls `coreapi` makes in order; a value named in one call and used in the next is passed as an argument. No context calls another (section 2.1). Reading commands begin with the open-record check of 5.2; plans end in `apply.Run` with the plan function and `format.Render` as its two arguments.

**validate (R).** `cli/cmd/format` → `coreapi.Validate` → `format.Load(tree, schema)` parses every rule file or guide block through `ordered` and `markdown`, validates each document against the format schema of the version the identity declares, resolves ids and aliases, checks the overlay and import contracts, runs the names check and the prose lint, compares each pin in identity (parent, imports) and each upstream pin (the rung-0 binding or the flattened set) with the pin the last upgrade entry in history recorded, listing "pin changed without its upgrade entry" and the state "behind its pins" (S64), computes stale translations → `model.Findings` and `model.Notices` → the root renders a listing. Any finding ends "findings", exit 1; a notice never changes the word (S27). A format newer than the engine ends "not ready" with both versions.

**The rule family (R).** Each is `cli/cmd/lifecycle` → one `coreapi` operation → `format.Load` → one `lifecycle.Plan*` function over the book and the clock → a `model.Delta` → `format.Render` → `apply.Run`. Where the plan must show the preset diff, `coreapi` first asks the tools' plugins for the presets of the book as it would be after the change and passes the diff in. `rule new` (a draft rule with its anatomy, S24's fields empty where the status allows); `rule edit` (fields that are not lifecycle); `rule replace` (a successor rule, the old one retired with a pointer); `rule retire` (status retired, successor optional); `rule restore` (a retired rule back to its last status); `rule renumber` (a new id with the old one kept as an alias, S3); `rule promote` (below); `rule confirm` (review-by moved, the reviewer recorded); `rule exclude` and `rule include` (an overlay's exclusion record with its sequence number and decision, or its lifting, S4; the plan reads the parent's bundle through `contract.ReadBundle` from the releases cache, and a parent not in the cache ends "not ready, parent not fetched", A50). Each ends "done" after a yes, "declined", or "refused" naming the field the guard would be bypassed on; `rule include` also ends "usage" on a rule the overlay has not excluded and "not ready" on one the parent retired (S4). M2's placement of a parent's bundle in the releases cache is one function, `coreapi.ParentBundle`: `releases/pinned/<the first sixteen hex digits of the sha256 of the pinned rulebook>/<version>/bundle.json` under the cache folder, read through a Tree root opened on that folder. The rulebook is named by its digest and the version must be a semantic version, so no pin's text is ever a path. M5's `release.Fetch` keeps this placement or moves it, in that function and nowhere else. `term`, `source` and `decision` follow the same shape with their own plan functions; `decision supersede` writes the new record and marks the old one, never editing it [docs.freshness.5].

**rule promote (R).** `coreapi.RulePromote(id, decision)` → `format.Load` → the plugins' presets of the book with the new status for the preset diff → `lifecycle.PlanPromote(book, id, decision, presetDiff, clock)` checks S24's required fields, the trial window and the governance, builds the status change and the history entry → `format.Render` → `apply.Run`. A missing source, pair or decision ends "refused" naming the field.

### 7.3 The contract: one schema source, generated types

*Designed, built in M1.*

The contract has its own semantic version (section 9 of the spec). Its source is one set of JSON Schemas per contract version under `internal/host/assets/contract/schema/vN/`, one per emitted document (bundle, report, changelog, SARIF, plan, proposal) and one per entity. From that one source, `cmd/gragas-dev` generates and `regenerate-and-diff` holds [supply.provenance.4, gates.own-rules.3]:

- Go types in `internal/model` (a generated file per document, marked)
- TypeScript types and zod schemas in `explorer/packages/contract` [ts.schema.1, ts.type-safety.4]
- the field reference skeleton the handbook completes with derivation rules
- the list of fields the explorer reads, checked against the schema in a test (consumer-driven) [arch.ports.7, web.structure.11]

The explorer's zod is generated, not hand-written, so there is one model and one schema library on the TypeScript side [ts.schema.1]. Every `--json` document names the contract version in one top-level field, `contract` [cli.output.1]. Documents are encoded by `canon` in the form of RFC 8785 (A59): keys sorted by their UTF-16 code units, the one number form of a binary64, LF, no map order leaks, so the same inputs give the same bytes (S57, section 13).

Compatibility: a minor adds fields and enum values with defaults; both shapes are emitted for two minors before a major removes one; the Go reader and the explorer read the current and previous major; `contract validate` checks any document and names both versions on a newer one (S25, domain 10).

### 7.4 Goldens and the fixture rulebook

*Designed, built in M1.*

It holds: a base fixture with every field exercised (two kinds, the four statuses, every standing and mark, every observation, Markdown and YAML forms, a translation, an exclusion, an import), a second fixture it maps to, three releases of history, two project fixtures (clean and weakened), and the documents those produce: the bundle, two reports, a changelog across three releases, a rule map, the renderings. ### 8.3 Routing and state

Hash history, so the page opens from a static host with no rewrite rule and, with its data inlined (8.4), from a local file, and an iframe's route never collides with its host's. Every route declares a zod schema for its search params, validated where they enter [react.routing.3]. Links use typed destinations [react.routing.2]. Every route has an error view and a not-found view set once [react.routing.6]. Route files hold route config plus one page import from `core`'s public entry; `main.tsx` only wires [react.routing.11].

### 8.4 Data loading

Exactly one module touches the network or writes a URL: `core/data/load.ts` [web.structure.1]. The root route's loader delegates to the query client [react.routing.5], which obtains `bundle.json` and, in standalone, `report.json`, parses each with the generated zod [web.states.6], and keeps both with infinite freshness from one policy file [react.server-state.2]. A page opened from a local file cannot fetch a sibling file, so the standalone packaging carries its data inside the page: the engine writes the bundle and the report into the HTML as JSON script blocks, the data module reads those blocks first and falls back to fetching for a hosted copy; the other packagings fetch (README ruling 8, A40). The inlined bundle leaves out the proof grid, which is the largest part and which no view needs beyond the marks the bundle already carries; the grid is written beside the page as `proof.json` and fetched where the page is hosted, and the handbook names the size (a ten-thousand-rule bundle without the grid is a few megabytes; above the limit the handbook names, `explorer` says so and writes the fetched form). No `useEffect` fetches [react.server-state.5]. Everything after the parse is a pure selector over the parsed documents, tested in node. Each data region declares one of the six states and draws each [web.states.1]: a missing bundle is an empty frame naming the file; a previous contract major loads with a notice; a report from another release shows a banner and a mark per differing row (domain 7). A value nobody read shows a dash with its reason [web.states.5]. Pages orchestrate, parts take plain props and import no data layer [react.components.1].

**The standalone page as files.** Browsers refuse module scripts and sibling fetches from a local file, so the standalone packaging is `index.html`, one classic (non-module) script file, one stylesheet and the data blocks, all written by `explorer` into the `out` folder. The script is built as a single self-contained file with no dynamic import, so the page has no inline script and no chunk to fetch; the data blocks are `application/json` and are never executed. The page ships a content security policy in a meta element with no `unsafe-inline` for script, no external origin, and fonts self-hosted [web.speed.4, web.speed.7, web.tokens.12]. The hosted packagings (`element`, the plugins) split the views by route so a host loads only what it shows, and development tooling never enters a production bundle [react.performance.2]. A byte budget per kind of file is measured on the built output and the check proves it can fail [web.speed.1, web.speed.5]. The decision is A47.

## 9. The terminal

*Designed, built in M0a.*

One voice for maintainer, developer and agent (domain "across all"). The look comes from the shared terminal kit, by version, imported only by `host/term` [cli.look.1]. Counts and sizes go through its helpers [cli.look.3]. One token file is the source of every terminal colour, generated from the same token document the explorer uses, and every text colour is contrast-tested [cli.look.2].

**Deep links.** The docs base URL in identity (S72) drives the docs profile's page pattern, `meta.docs.url` in the bundle and the preset's docs URL on checker-code rules where the tool allows one. The terminal prints a rule's page link, and its explorer link where the second URL is set, beside every `explain` and `show`; a rulebook with no base URL prints ids only, and the engine never derives an address from a repository host.

**Small terminal rules.** A pager is used only when stdin and stdout are both terminals, and a failing pager never loses the page [cli.environment.10]. The terminal is queried only to render, with a bounded wait [cli.environment.3]. A table keeps its columns, a long cell wraps, a dropped column is marked, and piped output has no rules [cli.look.7]. A number is shown no finer than its source resolves, and an estimate is marked as one [cli.output.10]. Output, help and docs say only what the code does: a verb or option decided and not built is a row in the registry's not-built list and absent from the command tree, a refused designed option names what this build has, and a test fails when a listed verb appears in the tree, so a stub cannot ship in its place [cli.commands.9]. This is how the milestones of section 18 add commands: a command enters the tree at its milestone and leaves the not-built list. A new or changed command or menu shows its terminal transcript in the change [cli.commands.16].

**Closing words and exit codes.** The words are a closed set, the type `model.Word`, built by constructors only [cli.look.5]: done 0, findings 1, failed 1, usage 2, busy 3, not ready 4, refused 5, declined 6. The spec's seven are outcomes of the work; "failed" is the eighth, the engine itself failing (an unreadable settings file, a git child that dies, a torn journal, a panic caught at the root), and it carries the run id. It shares code 1 with "findings" so a gate fails on either, which is the safe direction; the word and the `--json` kind tell them apart, and cli.exit.1 forbids only sharing a code with "refused" (README ruling 1, A39). `internal/exit` maps a word to a code. It declares all eight codes, gragas's own, since the kit's exit leaf declares none from go-run v0.5.0 on, and builds them into the kit's table of endings (`NewTable`, one kind per word), which go-run's `exit.New` checks; `main` checks that table before anything runs and stops on failed if it is not whole (gragas#1452). The waiver README ruling 3 recorded for the local 6 went with the kit's own codes [cli.exit.1, cli.exit.2, arch.shared.7]. Only `main` calls `os.Exit` [go.errors.4]. A command ends in exactly one word, printed by the root; piped, the word goes to stderr so `--json` keeps stdout clean [cli.output.1]. Every run reads the same way: a heading, what it reads, the work, the dry-run footer when nothing was written, one closing word [cli.commands.14].

**`--json`.** One document on stdout naming the contract version; everything else on stderr [cli.output.1, cli.output.7]. A command with no document of its own prints the shared result type: the word, the changes made or planned, the next line, an error with its kind, and for "failed" the run id [cli.output.6]. `--json`, `--plain`, `--accessible` and `--no-color` are registered once by the root [cli.output.5]. `--no-color` is the estate's spelling of the flag and the environment variable, and a lint exemption from docs.spelling.2 is recorded once for it. `--sarif` is available where a command lists findings (15.5). JSON and `--help` have golden snapshots the gate cannot rewrite [cli.output.3]. The command tree (commands, flags, types, defaults, exit codes, JSON field names) is written to a committed file and diffed against the last tag [cli.output.4]. A value the engine could not establish is null with a reason, never 0 or a dash [cli.output.9].

**Plain and accessible modes.** NO_COLOR, a non-terminal, `TERM=dumb`, CI and `--no-color` all give plain output; nothing is asked when nobody can answer, and each question names the flag that answers it in code [cli.environment.1, cli.environment.5, cli.environment.8, cli.environment.14]. Accessible mode adds the word and the symbol of section 17 beside every colour, plain questions, plain bars, no banner [cli.environment.7]. Plain output carries the same facts as coloured output [cli.environment.11]. Width is the terminal's, else COLUMNS, never above 96 [cli.environment.2].

**The bare menu by context.** `cli/root` detects rulebook, project, both or neither (section 6) and leads with that group (S19): a rulebook leads with validate, rule, bind, prove, release, attention; a project with status, explain, check, waive, upgrade, switch, report, agents; both shows both groups, rulebook first, with `upgrade` and `propose` in each; neither offers init (project or rulebook) and the handbook. The banner shows only in the menu. At a terminal the menu ends "done"; off a terminal the bare command prints help and ends "usage" (15.1) [cli.commands.12]. The menu never runs a changing command without the form. A mistyped command prints the nearest match on stderr with empty stdout and the usage code [cli.commands.10]. Commands follow `[group] verb [noun]` with the rule, term, source, decision, contract and plugin groups, the rest flat, all declared once in a registry that generates help and the README's command table [cli.commands.26]. The registry is one domain-verb table; it refuses the verbs get, make and destroy; and each entry carries two examples, the files the command writes, its exit words and its `--json` kind, so help can print them [cli.commands.8, cli.commands.26]. A new command comes from `gragas-dev new-command`, which scaffolds the command package below `internal/cli/cmd` with its row, the coreapi function and the test stubs from one golden command package, and a heavy test runs the generator into a copy of the tree and checks the result [arch.templates.2, tests.runners.11].

**Forms.** A form is the cli layer's: it collects answers, calls the one plan operation, shows the plan, asks once. A test walks every form [cli.environment.14]. The main journeys (first run, help, menu, one happy path, one refusal) run under a pty at 80 columns as goldens, and again at 60 and 40 held to properties against those goldens: no line wider than the screen, nothing cut off, the same rows in the same order. Both run at pre-push [cli.commands.15, tests.journeys.8].

## 10. Errors, logging and concurrency

**Errors.** Every operation returns a typed `model.Result` carrying a `model.Word`; a command never throws past the root, and a panic is a bug. The root recovers a panic into "failed" with the run id, since the facet `single-client` removes cli.errors.5 but not the need [go.errors.4, cli.errors.1]. It is the one recover in the program (#974). A command whose crash must read as a no carries the `fails-closed` annotation (`kit.FailsClosed`), and its panic ends "refused", exit 5, with the annotation's line to type. Only `guard edit` carries it, so an agent's hook never reads a crashed guard as a pass (#165). A refusal is one value, a word from the closed list plus a hint line to paste, built by a constructor that requires both [cli.errors.2, cli.errors.6]. A failure is the same shape with the word "failed", the kind of failure and the run id, never a refusal's code (section 9). Errors inside the engine are wrapped with `%w` and what was being done, compared with `errors.Is` and `errors.As`, handled once [go.errors.2, go.errors.7]. An error that crosses the process boundary (`--json`, the guard's answer) is a word from the closed list plus detail, typed again on the other side [go.errors.8]. Text the engine did not write (file names, rulebook strings, tool output) has control sequences stripped before it reaches a terminal [cli.errors.4]. An error shown to a person carries the run id [cli.errors.1].

**Logging.** The engine is not a daemon. A long run (prove, verify, a fetch) shows progress at a terminal and plain lines on stderr off one [cli.commands.13, cli.environment.9]. Every line of the journal and every stderr diagnostic carries the engine version and the run id from one handler [go.logging.2 is proposed; the design meets it]. Nothing is logged twice, and nothing is both logged and returned [go.errors.7].

**Concurrency.** Sequential by default [go.concurrency.7]. `context.Background` appears only in `main` and tests [go.concurrency.1]; SIGINT and SIGTERM cancel through `signal.NotifyContext`, and a cancelled apply rolls back from the journal [go.concurrency.4]. Adapters add no goroutine, poll or timer [go.concurrency.7]. Two places may add goroutines after a measured gain is cited at the `go` statement: `proof.Run` over independent tool versions, each a child process; and the gate's pool (`internal/dev/gate`, `Pool.Run`), which runs a tier's members side by side, each a make target in its own process; each package is tested with the race detector and a leak check [go.concurrency.6]. Every goroutine has an owner that waits for it [go.concurrency.2]. Writes are serialised through the Lock port, taken before the first write [cli.commands.3]. State is cleared when its cause ends: the scratch folder when the run ends, the lock on every path, the open mark when the record closes, and an explain-index entry when its lock digest is replaced [go.concurrency.5]. The run id comes from the cryptographic generator [supply.scans.6]. No check for a state that cannot happen, and no function that only forwards a call; pass-throughs are counted per package by a lint and read as a list [go.style.3].

## 11. Config and environment

*Designed, built in M0b.*

Settings come through one declared helper in `internal/config` that names the sources and their precedence: flags, then the environment, then the settings file, then defaults [cli.config.1]. The environment is read only in `config` and, for the terminal variables, in `host/term` [cli.config.3]. Paths come from one helper filled by the platform's defaults for a CLI (config, state, cache folders), one key each, overridden only by `--config` or one environment variable naming a file [cli.config.5].

The settings file is one YAML document, `<config>/gragas/settings.yaml`: `language`, `colour`, `programs` (the absolute path of each child program: git, the three sandbox programs, and the ssh and the credential helper git runs for a host, each refused when it holds a single quote or a control character, since git hands it to its shell; remote-host-fetch), `sandbox` (the adapter to use at the sandboxed level), `tools` (the path of the per-machine tools file), `tool-paths` (a tool's location on this machine, per project root), `mirrors` (a map from a rulebook source to a mirror), `keep` (the age the journal's copies and the caches are kept). Every key and environment variable has a reader, a default, a doc row and a refused-when [cli.config.6]. An unknown key in the file is refused naming it, and `signing-key` or `programs.signer`, which release-pin-by-hash took out, naming that reason; a missing file means defaults; an unreadable one is an error [cli.config.2, cli.config.10]. No secret comes from a flag and none is printed; the engine holds none (section 1) [cli.config.4, supply.secrets.2]. A value specific to one installation (a mirror, a tool path) comes only from the settings file, never from a literal [code.config.1].

The per-machine tools file (A8) maps `tool@version` to an absolute path. The rulebook's gate fills it the way the handbook shows; the engine never installs a tool (S23).

**Settings that name something to run.** The settings file and the tools file decide which programs the engine executes, so each is refused when another account owns it or when its group or others can write it, and the refusal gives the line that fixes the mode [cli.config.12, A48]. A setting that can be implemented more than one way names the implementation with one key and takes its options in a table named after it: `sandbox: container` with a `container:` table, or `userns`, or `macos`; an unknown value is refused naming the implementations this build has; a key that may grow accepts either its value or a table holding it, and the handbook documents both as one shape [cli.config.11]. A renamed or retired key is refused at its file and line with the migrate command. `migrate` covers the settings file as a plan: a dry run first, then it reads the new file back and checks that it means the same settings as the old before it writes, and it prints the whole way back [cli.config.8, cli.config.2]. Flags are defined in `cli/*` only and a library takes parameters [cli.config.7].

## 14. Supply chain and release

*Designed, built from M0a; the release recipe in M10.*

- **Pinned.** One package manager per language, pinned, installing from the lockfile only, exact versions, no install scripts [supply.install.1, supply.install.4, supply.install.6]. Go modules are verified against the checksum database at add time and the module cache is primed; after install the build, the type check and the tests run with the network blocked [supply.install.2]. A dependency younger than the release-age window is refused, and the package manager's cooldown is set [supply.age.1, supply.age.2]. Integrity and provenance of locked packages are verified [supply.age.4]. Every licence sits on one allowlist [supply.licences.1]; LICENSE and NOTICE sit at the root and every artifact that bundles third-party code ships a generated notices file [supply.licences.6].
- **Tools.** `tools.yaml` pins every check tool by version and checksum; the preflight runs each and reads its version back; a fetched program is verified before use [supply.provenance.7, supply.install.10].
- **The local gate.** The hosted runners hold one workflow of proof, `go vet` and `go test` on Linux and Windows for a pull request, and on macOS too for main and a tag; it holds no secret and is not the gate. `task pre-push` is the one gate command of a push; the hook runs it on the pushed tree within the tier's budget (section 13) [gates.command.1, gates.hooks.1, gates.hooks.4, gates.land.2]. Bypass flags are refused [gates.hooks.2, gates.hooks.5]; the hook files are copied into .git/hooks/ once per clone. Every operating system the engine ships for is vetted at push, one run per system listed in the Taskfile (`GOOS=<os> go vet ./...`, which type-checks and does not link), and cross-compiled and linked in the release tier, before the tag [supply.release.7, section 19 item 5]. Pre-push also runs the secret scan over history and the workflow linter [supply.secrets.1, supply.workflows.2]. The vulnerability scan and the licence check are members of `full`, which `landing` runs after pre-push on the merged tree before main, so the hook's list is a declared subset of the land job's [supply.scans.7, gates.command.1].
- **The release.** One recipe, from a clean annotated tag on main, that runs the full gate first on its exact tree, serving nothing from a stamp, and never pushes [supply.release.1, tests.selection.13]. The recipe's jobs carry a stated timeout, 20 minutes for the gate job, and a run that reaches it is cancelled [gates.command.8, tests.measures.3]. CGO off, `-trimpath`, a fixed build id, the same bytes from any directory [go.layout.4, supply.release.3]. Assets: one binary per OS and architecture with the embedded explorer and handbook, `SHA256SUMS`, an SBOM, provenance where the host offers it, the notices file [supply.release.9]. No signature: a release is pinned by its content hash, and `SHA256SUMS` lists each asset by digest. The explorer's marked copy is built by the explorer build target, which the recipe calls before the Go build, so it enters the two-build comparison. After the tag build and before publish, a step unpacks the archive, runs `version` and one start test, and compares digests [supply.release.6]. `--version` prints the tag, the commit and the toolchain [cli.exit.3]. The only workflow is the tag workflow: a gate job with a read-only token, then a publish job [supply.workflows.1, supply.workflows.3]. A dispatched run gates and never publishes.
- **The unpack step as built** (`gragas-release verify`, gragas#347): each archive held to its checksum line, entries confined to their folder, each unpacked binary held by digest to the binary goreleaser built for its system (read from the build's folder as goreleaser's artifacts.json names it, never from the archive), and the host's binary run with its own home and temp folder, behind go-proc's block (its sandbox package) with no network or proxy variable, and refused by name where `sandbox.Check` finds no block (gragas#1455): `version`, which prints the tag, `help`, and `contract fixture`, which writes the fixture rulebook. What it found is written to unpack.json beside the release folders, pass or fail, and is not shipped. [supply.release.6].
- **The explorer's packages** publish from the same tag with provenance, exact versions, and a peer range only where React is a peer [supply.install.4].
- **Release hygiene.** The release is refused on a declared-API break that has no declaration, checked against the surface listing (3.1) and the committed command tree before the tag [supply.release.2]. The changelog entry is plain words a person writes, with a proposed version a person may override, and it names the contract number [supply.release.5]. The engine installs and upgrades nothing, itself included [cli.commands.5]. The contract and the published packages are a shared source with a named owner (the operator, through the area lead), one home (this repository), a stated version (the contract's own) and a place to ask with a response time, all three stated in the README before the first release [arch.shared.14]. Forbidden names are scanned for from a hashed list kept outside the repository [supply.secrets.4].

## 18. Phasing

Milestones in build order. Each ends in something the owner can run, and each uses only what earlier milestones built. Each lands green under the gate as it stands at that point: from M0a to M5 the gate keeps running the current `check` and `gen` over `rulebook/` through the old commands mounted behind the new root (A41), and the owner's rulebook stays where it is until M6.

**M0a The skeleton.** First the contract test and the transcripts of the current commands (section 15). Then the layout of section 3, the `gate/` registry files of 13.2 with their schemas, the surface listing and the measure step, `layers.yaml` with its `contexts:` and `explorer:` tables rendering the boundary lint, `model` with the word type, `exit` over the kit's codes, `canon`, `ordered`, `markdown`, the thirteen ports and the fake (twelve since release signing went, thirteen again with Owned, gragas#1940), `host/clock`, `host/term`, `host/assets` with the placeholder page, `cli/root` with context detection, the bare menu, the eight words, `version`, `help`, `--json` and `--plain`, the registry with its not-built list and its committed command table, goldens, the old `check` and `gen` mounted behind the new root, `cmd/gragas-dev` with the layers generator, `surface`, `new-command` and `sync`, the managed root files laid down from the template of each kind (`cli`, `web-app`) with a lock file and the sync check in `task pre-push` (A51). Runs: `gragas`, `gragas version`, `gragas help`, and the old `gragas check` and `gragas gen` unchanged. One to two weeks.

**M0b The write path.** `host/fs` (Tree, Writer with append, Lock with the two-lock order, Journal), `internal/apply` with the input set, the intent, the goal predicate, roll-back, the open-record check, `journal` and `journal restore`, `cache`, the settings file and the path helper, the table of persisted files (5.5), `--accessible`, crash injection, the pty journeys at 80 columns. **M1 Format and the contract skeleton.** `core/format`: both source forms, `Render` with the round-trip golden, ids and aliases, the overlay and import contracts, the names check, the prose lint, translations, format schema v3, the rulebook form of `init`, which writes the first history entry (sequence number 1, no previous digest, the starting version and the plan digest, S67, S68) through `apply` and Writer's append, with `format.Load` reading `history.jsonl` and its entry shape (5.1) and the one `git config` call that gives "who" (the identity read of `host/git`; the rest of `host/git` is M2's); `migrate` from the owner's schema v2, which has no pins, so the old-form pin conversion of section 15 waits for M6. `core/contract`: the contract schemas, the generators for Go and TypeScript, the canonical encoder, the fixture rulebook with its hand-authored documents as goldens, `contract validate`, `contract fields`, `contract fixture`. ## 19. Where the spec still cannot be built as written

The earlier list had ten items. The spec at 77a7bd8 now carries nine of them: `check` in both contexts and the rulebook's gate listing (S14, 15.1), `guard` as the fifty-second command (domain 2, 15.1), the bare command ending "usage" off a terminal (15.1, flow 9), `propose` printing the document and the line that opens it (S5, S65, flows 5 and 7), release committing the named files and making the tag inside the yes and never pushing (domain 5, flows 1 and 2), the yes flag's meaning, verify's read-only access to the project's dependencies (S23, S44), the index as a file in a repository read by git (S11), and the counts. They are built here as described and are no longer listed. The spec at its S72 now also carries items 1 to 4 below (the eighth word in S19 and 15.4, domain 4's invariant, the typed name in S43, and `journal` and `cache` in 15.1, fifty-four commands); they stay listed with their wording until the architecture review marks them closed, and none blocks the build. What remains (items 5 and 6 are clashes between an active rule and an operator ruling, listed here because neither is bent silently):

1. **The exit table and the eighth word.** cli.exit.1 fixes the estate's codes at 0 to 5 and names 1 "failed"; cli.look.5 closes the kit's word set. The spec's S19 needs "declined" at 6 and has no word for the engine failing. The amendment is filed as rule-source/amendments#34: 6 declined added to cli.exit.1's set, the words findings, busy, not ready and declined added to the kit's closed set by a version bump, and "failed" sharing code 1 with "findings", told apart by the word and the `--json` kind. Spec fix: S19 and 15.1 name "failed" as the eighth word, "the engine itself failed; exit 1 like findings, so a gate fails; the word and the kind tell them apart". Until the amendment lands the engine carries the waivers of A42.
2. **Domain 4's invariant against the revised S23 and S44.** Domain 4 says "Nothing from a project enters a run". S23 and S44 now give verify's run read-only access to the project's own dependencies where the adapter names them. Spec fix: domain 4's invariant reads "Nothing from a project enters a run except, in verify, read-only access to the project's dependencies the adapter names (S23); nothing is executed from them and nothing is written under the project".
3. **`--confirm <name>` on destructive steps.** 15.1 and S43 describe the yes and name no second confirmation. cli.commands.3 requires the thing's own name for a destructive step, and README ruling 2 names withdraw, archive, switch and the two opt-outs. Spec fix: S43 adds "withdraw, archive, switch and the opt-out forms of agents and review also ask for the thing's own name, typed at a terminal or given as a flag", so the form's screen is in the spec and not only here.
4. **The run-level way back.** cli.commands.25 wants each run's record to keep the copies and print a way back. The spec has `undo` as a lifecycle correction and no run-level listing. Spec fix: 15.1 adds `journal` (both; a listing of this root's runs, and a mode that prints the lines that restore one run's files; done, or refused when a file moved since) and `cache` (any; a listing of the engine's caches with a clear mode; done), fifty-four named commands. Neither changes a rulebook's or a project's data.
5. **Cross-compilation at push.** supply.release.7 (active, Hard) says every shipped operating system is "compiled and vetted at push, from any host". Operator ruling 6 of the architecture review moved cross-compilation to the release tier under a three-minute pre-push budget. The design keeps the ruling for the link and the build, and runs only `GOOS=<os> go vet ./...` per shipped OS at push, a type-check with no link, which is what the rule's own detail asks for. Whether that reading of ruling 6 is acceptable is the operator's call. Resolution proposed: accept it; if the three-minute budget cannot hold with the per-OS vet, the owner either raises the budget by the measured cost or amends supply.release.7 to say "vetted at push for the host's OS and in the release tier for the rest".
6. **The pre-push budget against the checks the rules now put there.** The pre-push tier of section 13 carries the unit tests with the race detector, the explorer's component tests in two browser engines, the admission ladder over changed tests, the per-OS vet, and the vulnerability, secret, licence, forbidden-name and workflow scans, under ruling 6's three minutes. The budget is a measured number (tests.measures.2); the first measurement is taken at M0b and printed with each run. Resolution proposed: move checks to the release tier in this order until the budget holds, never skip one: the admission ladder's mutation step, the WebKit pass of the component tests, the history-wide secret scan. If the per-OS vet and the plants alone exceed the budget, the operator's ruling 6 is raised again with the measured numbers.
